# A self-referencing robust threshold cannot detect a ramp at any magnitude

John Hagedorn

## Summary

A robust z-score that computes its median and median absolute deviation from
the same series it is scoring has a hard upper bound on the score it can ever
produce for a linear ramp. That bound is

    max|z| = 0.5 / (0.25 * 1.4826) = 1.3489815...

and it does not depend on the size of the ramp. A ramp that rises by a factor
of 1000 produces exactly the same maximum score as one that rises by a factor
of 2. Any alert threshold above about 1.35 is therefore unreachable on ramp
data by construction, and the conventional choice of 3 can never fire.

This note derives the bound, verifies it numerically, establishes the exact
conditions under which it holds, and shows that computing the median and MAD
over a declared stationary reference window removes the problem. Every number
quoted here comes from `results/` in this repository and is regenerated by
`python -m tsadharness.cli run --out results/`.

## 1. The rule as it is usually written

The rule under examination appears in a great many monitoring pipelines in
roughly this form:

```python
med = np.median(x)
mad = 1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(x - med))
z = np.abs(x - med) / mad
alert = z > 3
```

The factor 1.4826 is the normal-consistency constant: for Gaussian data,
1.4826 times the MAD is an unbiased estimator of the standard deviation, which
is what allows the threshold to be interpreted as "three sigma". The appeal of
the rule is that the median and the MAD are both robust, with a breakdown point
of 50%, so a handful of outliers cannot corrupt the reference statistics the
way a mean and a standard deviation would.

The defect is not in the robustness. It is that `med` and `mad` are computed
from `x`, the same array being scored. The rule has no notion of a healthy
period. It asks only whether each point is unusual relative to its own series.

## 2. Derivation

Consider a series that is a linear ramp of range R, sampled uniformly. The
values are uniformly distributed on an interval of width R. Write them as
`x = a + R*t` for `t` uniform on [0, 1].

The median is the midpoint, `med = a + R/2`.

The deviations from the median, `d = x - med = R*(t - 1/2)`, are uniformly
distributed on `[-R/2, +R/2]`. Their absolute values `|d|` are uniformly
distributed on `[0, R/2]`, so the median of the absolute deviations is the
midpoint of that interval:

    MAD = R/4

The scaled MAD is therefore

    1.4826 * R/4 = 0.37065 * R

The largest absolute deviation from the median is attained at either endpoint:

    max|d| = R/2 = 0.5 * R

Dividing gives the maximum attainable score:

    max|z| = (0.5 * R) / (0.37065 * R) = 0.5 / 0.37065 = 1.3489815...

R cancels. This is the entire result. The numerator and the denominator are
both proportional to the range of the ramp, so their ratio is a pure number.
Making the fault larger enlarges the estimated scale by exactly the same factor
as it enlarges the deviation being scored, and the two effects annihilate.

The residual is exactly zero, not merely small, across three orders of

With noise added and a leading flat baseline, the measured ceiling approaches
the constant from above as R grows, because at small R the additive noise is a
non-negligible fraction of the total spread. From `results/sweep.csv`, the mean

At no magnitude does the score come within a factor of two of the threshold of
3. The measured detection rate for `MADZScore` on `linear_ramp` is 0.00 at
every one of the ten magnitudes tested, from 1.5x to 1000x.

## 4. The conditions under which the bound holds

The derivation above assumes the scored window is entirely ramp. Real scoring
windows usually open with some flat healthy data, so it matters how much
baseline the result tolerates.

Let a fraction `b` of the window be flat baseline at the ramp's starting value,
and the remaining `1-b` be the ramp. For `b < 1/2` the median still falls
inside the ramp, at `R(0.5-b)/(1-b)` above the baseline. The absolute
deviations below that median come from both the baseline block and the lower
part of the ramp, and working through the cumulative distribution gives

    MAD = 0.25*R/(1-b)     provided  b <= 1/4
    max|d| = 0.5*R/(1-b)

The `(1-b)` factors cancel along with R, so

    max|z| = 0.5/(0.25*1.4826) = 1.3489815...

exactly as before, for every `b` up to 1/4. Above `b = 1/4` the baseline block
becomes large enough to pull the MAD below `0.25*R/(1-b)`, and the ceiling
rises. Measured on a 200001-point noiseless series:

| baseline fraction b | 0.00 | 0.05 | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.25 | 0.30 | 0.40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| max\|z\| | 1.349 | 1.349 | 1.349 | 1.349 | 1.349 | 1.686 | 3.372 |

So the honest statement of the result is: **the bound holds whenever the ramp
occupies at least three quarters of the scored window.** The effective
breakdown point for a ramp is 1/4, not the 1/2 that the median and MAD enjoy
against point outliers. This is the practically relevant regime for condition
monitoring, where the analyst is typically looking at a trailing window of the
recent past and the asset has been degrading throughout it.

The corresponding figure for a step change is 1/2: a step is detected easily at
any contamination fraction except exactly one half, where the median lands in
the gap between the two levels and every deviation is identical. That contrast
is worth stating, because it locates the defect precisely. The rule is not
broken in general. It is broken on a particular geometry.

## 5. The result is about uniformity, not about gradualness

It would be easy to over-generalise this into "robust z-scores cannot see slow
change". That is false, and the harness contains the counterexample.

An exponential ramp rising to the same final value is detected. Its values are
log-uniformly rather than uniformly distributed, so they bunch towards the
bottom of the range; the median is dragged well below the mid-point while the
largest deviation still tracks the top of the range. The two therefore no
longer scale together. Measured on noiseless 1000-point series:

| magnitude | linear max\|z\| | exponential max\|z\| |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 1.348 | 2.014 |
| 5 | 1.348 | 2.583 |
| 10 | 1.348 | 3.755 |
| 100 | 1.348 | 13.781 |

`MADZScore` detects the exponential ramp from about 10x upward, where the score
first clears 3, and its measured detection rate rises from 0.00 to 1.00 across
that boundary.

The blindness is thus a property of the **uniform distribution of values** that
a linear ramp produces, which is the case in which the MAD scales in exact
proportion to the extreme deviation. A linear ramp is the worst case, and it is
also the shape most often used to model steady wear.

## 6. The fix, and what it costs

The structural correction is to compute the median and the MAD over a declared
stationary reference window and freeze them, rather than recomputing them from
the data being scored. In this repository that is `MADReference`, which differs
from `MADZScore` in no other respect: same functional form, same threshold of
3, same input.

With a reference window set to the first 20% of the series, the measured
detection rate on `linear_ramp` is **1.00 at every magnitude from 1.5x to
1000x**, against 0.00 for the self-referencing version. The same holds on
`seasonal_plus_ramp` from 2x upward and on `ramp_with_gaps` at every magnitude.
This is the headline figure of the repository, `results/magnitude_sweep.png`.

The fix is not free, and the false-positive table records the cost. Because the
frozen scale is now a small number derived from quiet data, `MADReference`
fires on 95% of pure-noise series at least once, against 90% for `MADZScore`;
a fixed 3-sigma rule crosses roughly 0.27% of Gaussian samples, so over 1000
points at least one crossing is close to certain. More seriously, on
`regime_switch` -- a healthy asset alternating between two normal operating
levels -- `MADReference` flags 50% of all points, because the second operating
mode lies far outside the reference window. `MADZScore` flags none of them,
precisely because it adapts to whatever it is shown.

That inversion is the real trade. The self-referencing rule is immune to benign
regime changes for the same reason it is blind to ramps: it has no fixed notion
of normal. Choosing between them is a choice about which error you would rather
make, and it cannot be made well without measuring both, which is what this
harness is for. The accompanying disjoint-window control exists to catch the
specific failure the reference-window approach introduces, by retraining on a
non-overlapping window and checking whether the two models agree about the
error level.

## 7. Why this matters

Gradual degradation is the thing condition-monitoring programmes most want to
detect. Bearings, filters, heat exchangers and seals overwhelmingly fail by
progressive drift rather than by discontinuity, and the value of monitoring
lies in seeing that drift early enough to schedule work.

A detector built on a self-referencing robust threshold cannot report that
class of fault. Not "reports it late", not "reports it with low sensitivity" --
cannot report it, at any severity, because the score is bounded below the
threshold by construction. Such a system will nonetheless look healthy on a
dashboard: it will fire correctly on steps, spikes and sensor dropouts, produce
a low false-positive rate, and give every appearance of working. Its silence on
the failure mode of interest is indistinguishable from an absence of faults.

This is a small, concrete instance of the argument made by Wu and Keogh, that
current time-series anomaly detection benchmarks are flawed and are creating an
illusion of progress [1]. Their case rests largely on benchmark datasets being
trivially solvable, mislabelled, and dominated by point anomalies, and on the
widespread use of point-adjusted scoring, under which a single correct point
inside an event is credited with the whole event. This harness reproduces that
last effect directly: a uniform random scorer achieves a point-adjusted F1
above 0.9 on a long event while its honest point-wise F1 is below 0.1.

The present result adds a different kind of example to the same argument. Here
the benchmark is not mislabelled and the metric is not the problem. The
detector is structurally incapable of the detection, and no amount of
evaluation on step-and-spike benchmarks would reveal it, because on those
geometries the detector genuinely works. The failure is invisible unless the
evaluation set deliberately contains the geometry that breaks it -- which is
the argument for building the evaluation set from generators with known ground
truth, and for keeping a random baseline and a no-anomaly control in every
table.
